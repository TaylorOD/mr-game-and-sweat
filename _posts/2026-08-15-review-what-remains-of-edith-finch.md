The Finch house is a good place for an easy treadmill walk, because you spend a lot of time exploring at your own pace instead of reacting to things coming at you. What Remains of Edith Finch is mostly a story you move through, so I’d pick it when I want the game to keep me company rather than make me work hard.

## Gameplay

I think it’s a lovely, unusual game, though it’s also very willing to slow down and let you listen. As Edith, you explore the family house and uncover stories about the people who lived there, and the house itself is full of little details that make poking around feel worthwhile. I’d be happy taking my time with it. It’s not exactly a pace setter.

The stories change how you interact with the game, which keeps the experience from being one long walk down a hallway. Lewis’s story has you working at a cannery while his imagination takes over, and that shift between the repetitive task and the fantasy is one of the more memorable ideas here. I think it’s clever without feeling like the game is showing off, although I’d expect to pay attention to the story instead of treating the cannery part as a mindless button press.

Some of the other stories are short and strange in a way that sticks with you, like Gregory’s bath and Calvin’s swing. They’re built around small actions, and the emotional weight comes from what you understand about the family, not from a difficult challenge. I don’t know that every story will land the same way for you, but the variety kept me interested just thinking about it. The game trusts you to connect some of the dots.

There are stretches where you’re mostly walking and listening, and if you’re hoping for constant activity, this probably isn’t going to hold your attention in the same way an action game would. The family history is the point, so skipping past the narration would be like putting on a movie and staring at the treadmill display. I’d rather go slowly and take it in, which is not usually how I want to spend a workout.

### Category Score: 2 out of 3

## Treadmill and Bike Compatibility

The game’s slow pace is a real advantage when you’re moving, because you can explore without needing quick aim or perfectly timed turns, and the full controller support means you can keep your hands where they need to be. I’d use a comfortable pace and let the walking sections set the rhythm. You’re not trying to win a race against the house.

Some moments ask for more attention than the rest, and Lewis’s cannery sequence is a good example, since you’ll be doing a repeated action while the story keeps changing around it. I’d probably ease off the pace during scenes like that, mostly because I’d want to follow what’s happening rather than turn the fish into a blur of missed inputs. I can only do one slightly odd task at a time.

On a bike, this seems similarly manageable, especially if you’re fine with a quieter workout and don’t need the game to keep your legs sprinting. I’d skip the urge to rush through the house, since the small details and shifts between stories are much of what make it work. There are no frantic fights waiting around the corner, which is nice when the controller is already doing a little bounce.

### Category Score: 3 out of 3

## Computer Requirements

The minimum graphics requirement is an older dedicated card, so this shouldn’t need a new gaming computer, though I’d check that your graphics hardware is at least around the listed GTX 750 or Radeon 7790 level. The processor and memory requirements are modest too, which leaves you with fewer things to worry about than the family tree.

### Category Score: 3 out of 3

## Conclusion and Score

This is for you if you want a thoughtful, story focused game to carry an easy workout, and probably not for you if you need constant action to keep your pace up. I’d play it on a treadmill or bike when I had room to slow down and listen. It’s a special little game.

### Overall Score: 2.67 out of 3
