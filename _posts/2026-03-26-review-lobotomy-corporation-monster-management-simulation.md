A shift at Lobotomy Corporation sounds like a decent treadmill distraction until one of your carefully chosen workers turns into a problem you have to deal with right now. I’d keep the pace easy, because this is more about watching a dangerous workplace than keeping your hands busy every second.

## Gameplay

The work assignments are what make this game interesting to me, because each creature has its own rules and you’re trying to get useful energy out of people doing jobs that can go very badly. You send an employee to work with an abnormality, check what happened, and decide whether to risk another attempt or leave it alone. It’s a simple idea that gets more tense as your facility fills up. I like that the monsters don’t feel like interchangeable energy machines.

There’s a real satisfaction to learning what a creature responds to, but I think the game asks you to remember a lot while also keeping an eye on the facility. You’re managing employees, trying to meet the day’s energy target, and watching for trouble when something gets loose. I’d expect the little decisions to keep my attention, though I’d also expect to pause and think more than I would in a game built around constant action. The clipboard is doing some work here.

The horror atmosphere helps. Even a familiar work order can feel questionable when the thing on the other side of it is called One Sin and Hundreds of Good Deeds, and I like that the game can make a routine choice feel a little off without turning every moment into a jump scare. It’s a strange office sim in the way a workplace with containment cells probably should be. I wouldn’t call it relaxing.

When an abnormality breaches containment, the game shifts from careful planning to getting employees through an immediate mess, and I think that change is effective even if it can be rough when you’re still learning. A mistake can spread, and losing a trained worker is more annoying than losing a disposable pawn because you’ve invested time in building them up. The roguelite structure gives you room to try again, but it doesn’t make every setback feel like a fun surprise. Sometimes you just have to clean up.

I’d expect the slower stretches to be the part that wears on me. You’re making work choices, checking results, and planning for the next round, so there are times when the game asks for patience instead of giving you something lively to react to. Still, the unusual creatures and the pressure of keeping the facility in shape give those stretches a reason to exist. I don’t know that I’d want to rush through it. You’re meant to pay attention.

### Category Score: 2 out of 3

## Treadmill and Bike Compatibility

On a treadmill, I’d treat this as a walking game rather than something to play while running hard, because the interface asks you to point at small bits of the facility and read what’s happening. A comfortable pace would leave room to make decisions without constantly losing your place on screen. I’d get the day organized before speeding up, and I’d slow down again when things go wrong. There’s no need to make the containment problem a balance problem too.

The game’s pause feature is useful for exactly the sort of moment where you need a second to think, and I’d use it freely instead of trying to issue orders while the facility is unraveling. That makes a bike feel more manageable than a brisk treadmill pace for me, since I’d expect less movement through my hands and upper body. Still, pauses don’t remove the need to read the situation and decide what to do. You can’t outsource the thinking to the bike.

I wouldn’t count on a controller making the interface comfortable without checking your setup first. This is a lot of pointing at employees and creatures, and those small selections are the sort of thing that can get fiddly when your arms are moving. If your setup lets you map controls in a way that feels natural, great, but I’d test it at an easy pace before committing to a long session. The mouse seems like the safer bet.

I’d also skip trying to play it during a hard run, because the interesting moments ask you to watch several parts of the facility and respond with some care. A breach can turn your calm little work shift into a scramble, and I’d rather be able to pause or slow down than try to make precise decisions while bouncing around. For a steady walk or an easy ride, though, the stop and start rhythm could fit pretty well. Just keep the intensity modest.

### Category Score: 1 out of 3

## Computer Requirements

This doesn’t look like a game that needs a recent graphics card, and the minimum listed card is old enough that I’d check the processor and memory before worrying about visuals. If your computer meets those minimums, the graphics side should be manageable. The office may be full of monsters, but it isn’t asking your PC to render a movie.

### Category Score: 3 out of 3

## Conclusion and Score

Lobotomy Corporation is for you if you like tense management games and don’t mind stopping to learn how the creatures behave. I’d give it a try on an easy walk or a relaxed bike ride, but I wouldn’t choose it for a workout where you want to move fast and think later.

### Overall Score: 2 out of 3
