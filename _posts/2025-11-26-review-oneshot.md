OneShot is a better fit for an easy bike ride than a hard treadmill run, since its puzzles ask you to stop and think instead of react quickly. Its odd desktop based puzzles can also pull your eyes away from the game, which is a less appealing trick when you are moving.

## Gameplay

OneShot is a top down puzzle adventure about guiding Niko, a child carrying a light bulb that serves as the sun, through a world that has been without one for a long time. You explore places such as the Barrens, the Glen, and the Refuge, talk to the people who live there, collect useful objects, and work out how to move forward. The central goal is clear, but the world’s characters and its strange rules give the trip more personality than a simple fetch quest.

The main activity is a steady mix of wandering, reading, and solving puzzles. You might search a room for something you can use, combine items, or follow a chain of clues to figure out how a machine works. Some puzzles are satisfying little leaps of logic. Others can leave you walking around and checking the same corners again, waiting for the one detail you missed to click. There is no rush to react, which is nice, though a few puzzles do ask for patience rather than quick progress.

The game’s biggest trick is that the world knows you exist. Niko is not simply talking to an invisible audience in the usual way. OneShot sometimes asks you to look beyond the game window for clues, and its puzzles can involve files or information on your computer. That makes the story feel unusual in a way that suits its lonely, worn down setting. It also means you may need to pause your workout to inspect something carefully. If you prefer games that keep your hands busy every minute, this one has a fair bit of reading and thinking between the more active stretches of exploration.

Niko is the emotional center of the story, and the game does a good job of making the journey feel like it matters without burying you in action. Dialogue and quiet scenes take up a meaningful amount of time. They add context and give the characters room to breathe, but they also slow the pace. You will probably spend more time considering what a line or clue means than dodging danger. That makes OneShot engrossing as a story and a puzzle game, but only moderately distracting as exercise entertainment.

### Category Score: 2 out of 3

## Treadmill and Bike Compatibility

The basic controls are simple: move around, examine things, and interact. There is no demanding combat or precision platforming, and most puzzles give you time to think. That is a strong starting point for playing while exercising. A bike at a gentle pace is the more comfortable match, since reading dialogue and studying clues takes your attention away from your surroundings.

The catch is that OneShot is not always contained to ordinary movement and interaction. Some puzzles ask you to check something outside the main game view, and those moments are awkward if your hands are busy or your screen is hard to see while moving. Even in the regular game, small details can matter, so a wobbly treadmill session may make exploring and checking objects more of a chore. You can take things at your own speed, but this is a game that rewards looking closely, not glancing at the screen between strides.

Its lack of twitch action keeps it playable during a workout, but the reading and desktop puzzles keep it from being an easy recommendation for a fast run. Set the pace low, keep the controls within easy reach, and be ready to pause when a puzzle needs your full attention.

### Category Score: 2 out of 3

## Computer Requirements

The minimum requirements are Windows Vista or later, 4 GB of memory, and an OpenGL 2.1 compatible graphics card. Those are modest requirements by current standards. The game’s simple top down presentation does not call for a modern gaming computer, and a basic integrated graphics setup should be enough for most machines that meet the listed requirements.

### Category Score: 3 out of 3

## Conclusion and Score

OneShot is a thoughtful puzzle adventure with a memorable central idea and a story that treats the player as part of its world. Exploring with Niko is pleasant, and its puzzles give you more to do than simply follow a marker from one room to the next. The tradeoff is a slow pace, plenty of reading, and occasional moments where you need to leave the normal game view to solve something.

For exercise, think of it as a low intensity companion rather than a game that will carry you through a fast workout. It works best when you can keep a steady pace, stop to read, and give tricky clues your attention. If that sounds fine, OneShot makes a good case for getting fit while gaming. Take that mom and dad!

### Overall Score: 2.33 out of 3
