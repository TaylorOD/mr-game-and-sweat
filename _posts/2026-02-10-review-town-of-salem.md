Save Town of Salem for a slow walk, because trying to read accusations and keep your feet moving at the same time sounds like a good way to miss both the vote and the belt. I think it’s a fun game, but it asks for more attention than I want to give a workout game.

## Gameplay

The fun is in trying to work out who’s lying while everybody else is trying to work out whether you’re lying, and I like that the whole group has to make decisions with incomplete information. A quiet player might be suspicious, or they might just be thinking, and that means even saying nothing can turn into evidence. That’s a pretty good setup for making a simple conversation feel tense.

The day and night rhythm gives the game a nice shape, but it also means you spend a lot of time reading and waiting for other people to talk. At night, your role may give you something specific to do, and during the day you’re comparing stories, making accusations, or trying not to look like you’re hiding something. I think that works well when you’re focused on the game. It’s less great when your legs are moving and a chat message just appeared that changes everything.

There’s a lot to learn because roles don’t all have the same goals, and the way you interpret one person’s behavior depends on what they might be. I don’t know that the learning curve is a dealbreaker, but there’s enough to keep straight that I’d probably want to play a few rounds sitting down before adding exercise. Trying to memorize who claimed what while also remembering what your own role can do is a little like keeping track of a group text where everyone is allowed to accuse you of murder.

I enjoy games that give you room to think, and this one has plenty of that, but some of the thinking time is just waiting for the other players to finish their turn or make up their minds. You can’t really speed that part along without missing the social deduction that makes the game work. It’s also the sort of game where one convincing accusation can send the whole group in the wrong direction, which is funny when you’re watching it happen and less funny when you realize you agreed with it.

The murder mystery idea is a strong fit for a party game, and the deception gives every round a reason to pay attention. Still, the reading and discussion are a big part of the experience, not a short pause between exciting parts. I’d play it when I wanted to think and talk, not when I wanted a game to keep my brain busy without needing my eyes.

## Treadmill and Bike Compatibility

A slow walk is the version I’d try first, with the treadmill speed low enough that reading chat doesn’t turn into a balance exercise. The game asks you to follow what people are saying, remember claims, and respond at the right time, so it’s not a good match for a hard run where your attention is already split. I’d expect the belt to keep going while I stop thinking about my pace entirely.

On a bike, the steady movement might be easier to pair with the slower parts, but the discussion still asks you to look at the screen and type. A stationary bike seems more reasonable than a treadmill for that, mostly because there’s less chance that glancing down at chat makes your feet do something surprising. I’d keep the resistance comfortable and make the game the thing I’m paying attention to.

I wouldn’t go into a round expecting to pause whenever I need to catch up, because this is a multiplayer game built around people talking and making decisions together. Missing a message could leave you behind, and then you’re trying to work out what happened while everyone else is already voting. That’s a lot to manage when your hands are bouncing. I’d skip it for intervals.

The text based discussion also makes the controls matter in a way that’s different from a game where you can steer with a thumb and keep moving. I’d use whichever input setup lets me type and select options comfortably, and I wouldn’t count on being able to do that cleanly while running. I can’t tell you whether a controller would feel good here, but the chat is what I’d plan around.

Maybe a very easy walk could work once you know the game and don’t need to stop for every new role, but even then, I’d expect to miss details now and then. Town of Salem rewards noticing what people said earlier, and a distracted accusation can get your whole plan sent in a bad direction. I’d rather save the more serious rounds for when I’m sitting still. My legs can wait.

## Computer Requirements

The listed minimums are modest, so I’d expect an older computer with basic integrated graphics to be enough for this one. I’d pay more attention to having a reliable internet connection than to buying a new graphics card, because this is a game about people responding to each other, not fancy visuals.

### Category Score: 3 out of 3

## Conclusion and Score

Town of Salem is worth trying if you enjoy social deduction and don’t mind reading, waiting, and arguing your case. For exercise, I’d keep it to an easy walk or a relaxed bike ride, and skip it when you want a game that lets you zone out and just move.

### Overall Score: 2 out of 3
