Keep the belt at a walking pace for Little Nightmares II, because its quiet sneaking can fit a workout but the sudden chases will ask for more attention than your legs probably want to give. I’d save the faster pace for a game that doesn’t occasionally make you jump at a teacher with a very long neck.

## Gameplay

The scale of the world does a lot of work here, and I think it’s more effective than making every room noisy and dangerous. You’re small, ordinary objects look huge, and moving through a place that feels wrong can be tense even when nothing is chasing you. That’s good for a workout because there’s usually something to watch for, even in the quieter stretches.

Mono and Six spend a lot of time sneaking, climbing, and solving environmental puzzles, and those parts give your brain something to do without filling the screen with menus or dialogue. Sometimes you’ll need to look around and figure out what the game expects, though, and I can imagine standing on a treadmill at a careful walk while I stare at a door and try to notice the one thing I missed. It’s not a terrible use of time. It’s just not especially athletic.

The school is where the game’s odd details really work for me. The teacher’s long neck is an unsettling thing to plan around, and the smaller enemies make the rooms feel busy in a way that’s fun to navigate. I like that the horror comes from how ordinary the places almost look, because then you’re sneaking through a classroom instead of staring at another hallway full of spooky lighting. Class was apparently not going well.

There are stretches where the game changes from careful sneaking to running, jumping, and trying not to get caught, and those moments are exciting in a way that also makes the controls feel less forgiving. The hospital’s mannequins are a good example of the kind of threat that makes you watch closely and move with purpose. I don’t know how much I’d enjoy retrying those parts while bouncing along, but at a slow pace they’d probably keep me awake.

The combat is clumsy by design, with Mono using objects as weapons, and I think that makes the encounters feel frantic rather than slick. Sometimes that fits the small, scared character you’re controlling, and sometimes it means a swing or a jump needs more precision than I’d want to handle while exercising. The story also leaves plenty unsaid, which suits the strange mood, though I wouldn’t pick this expecting a tidy explanation for every disturbing image.

## Treadmill and Bike Compatibility

Full controller support takes care of the basic setup, but this isn’t a game I’d pair with a hard run. The slow sneaking parts should be manageable on a treadmill or bike, and the quiet gaps between dangers give you room to settle into a rhythm, but chases and jumps can demand quick corrections. You may be doing fine one moment and then suddenly trying to steer Mono while your hands are moving with your steps. That’s a lot to coordinate.

I’d use a walking pace on the treadmill, or an easy effort on a bike, and slow down further when a chase begins. The game has checkpoints, so a mistake shouldn’t mean repeating a huge stretch, but having to retry a jump is still going to interrupt your workout. I’d pause when I need to work out a puzzle instead of letting the belt turn that into a moving concentration exercise.

The bike may feel easier during the puzzle sections because your upper body can stay more settled, but I wouldn’t call it automatically better. The school and hospital both have moments where turning, jumping, or reacting quickly matters, and those can be awkward on either machine if you’re pushing the pace. I’d probably keep the controller in a comfortable position and leave the intensity low enough that I can make small adjustments.

This also isn’t a game I’d choose for a workout where you want to zone out. You need to pay attention to the scenery, because it often tells you where to go or when a threat is close, and then the game occasionally turns that slow scan into a short panic. I think that contrast is part of what makes it memorable, but it means you should be willing to pause or slow down rather than trying to power through every section. It’s a better match for a careful workout than a fast one.

### Category Score: 2 out of 3

## Computer Requirements

The minimum graphics card is old enough that this doesn’t look like a game demanding a new gaming computer, and the store estimates 1080p at 60 frames per second on low settings, with drops possible in demanding scenes. I’d check the graphics card first if you’re on an older machine, since that’s the part most likely to decide whether those tense chase scenes stay smooth.

### Category Score: 3 out of 3

## Conclusion and Score

Little Nightmares II is a good choice for a slower workout if you want a creepy puzzle adventure that keeps asking you to pay attention. I’d skip it for hard running or any session where you want to play without adjusting your pace, but for an easy walk or ride, it should fit.
