Before you start a workout, I’d put No Rest for the Wicked on a day when you’re okay paying attention, because its fights ask for more than pointing your character toward the nearest enemy. The hand crafted world is lovely to look at, but you may spend more time concentrating on your feet and your screen than you planned.

## Gameplay

The combat is the reason I’d keep playing, and also the reason this isn’t an easy game to half pay attention to. Attacks have weight, stamina matters, and dodging at the wrong moment can turn a tidy fight into a short walk back to where you were. I like that the game wants you to pay attention to what an enemy is doing instead of running in and mashing buttons, but sometimes I’m just trying to get through a room, not have a conversation with a skeleton using a sword.

Warrick the Torn, the early boss, gives you a pretty clear idea of the deal. You have to watch his attacks and choose when to get in, which is a good way to make a fight feel like a fight, and maybe not the best way to keep your running rhythm relaxed. I’d expect boss encounters to take more focus than the regular exploring, so they’ll likely be the moments when your workout becomes a little less automatic.

There’s plenty to do between fights, and I think that helps the game feel like more than a series of boss doors. Exploring the world and gathering things for equipment gives you a reason to look around, though menus and inventory decisions can turn into a little pause in the middle of your session. I don’t know how much time I’d want to spend sorting gear while the treadmill keeps moving, but you can probably leave some of that for when you’re done.

The world has a nice, worn down look, and the art makes me want to see what’s around the next corner. That can pull you along without needing a long cut scene to explain why you should care, which is good for a workout. I’d still expect to stop and think sometimes, especially when a new enemy or tougher fight makes it clear that charging ahead is not a plan.

It’s Early Access, so you’re buying into a game that’s still being developed, and that’s part of the decision here. The core combat has enough personality to make me interested, but I’d be more cautious if you want a finished, polished adventure that won’t change as it develops. If you like learning a game while it’s still taking shape, that may be fine. I’d just go in knowing what you’re paying for.

## Treadmill and Bike Compatibility

A bike sounds easier for this one, but I don’t think the treadmill is out of the question. The camera angle gives you a clear view of the action, and you’re not aiming at a tiny target across the screen, which takes some pressure off. Still, you need to move, dodge, and time attacks with enough care that I’d start at a comfortable pace rather than treat the belt like background noise.

Controller play is the way I’d set it up. The combat depends on deliberate inputs, but it isn’t the kind of game where you need to whip the camera around constantly, and that’s a point in its favor. I’d probably lower the difficulty if the game gives you that option, though I wouldn’t count on a setting like that being available. If you’re not sure, start with a short, easy session and see whether your hands can keep up with your legs.

Boss fights are where I’d be most likely to slow down or pause the workout. When Warrick starts demanding close timing, it’s hard to imagine staying relaxed if you’re also trying to keep your stride even, and I’d rather take a breath than turn a game into an argument with my own thumbs. Regular exploration seems more workable, but I’d still expect a surprise enemy to make you focus quickly.

The inventory is another reason I’d set a boundary before starting. It’s tempting to stop and compare equipment whenever you pick something up, and that’s the sort of small decision that can leave you standing on a moving belt doing absolutely no exercise. I’d handle gear before or after a session and keep the actual workout for exploring and fighting.

On a bike, I think the seated position gives you a little more room to focus on careful timing, especially if you keep the resistance comfortable. I don’t know that I’d want to play it during an intense effort, because the game already has moments that ask for attention and you don’t need both parts trying to be the boss. A steady ride seems reasonable. A hard interval session probably isn’t.

### Category Score: 2 out of 3

## Computer Requirements

This needs a dedicated graphics card, and integrated graphics are specifically not supported, so I’d check the GPU before anything else. The listed minimum is an older but still capable gaming card, which puts this beyond a basic laptop but doesn’t mean you need a brand new high end computer.

### Category Score: 2 out of 3

## Conclusion and Score

No Rest for the Wicked is a good fit for a steady bike ride or an easy treadmill session if you enjoy deliberate combat and don’t mind pausing for tougher fights. I’d skip it for workouts where you need the game to stay simple and hands off.

### Overall Score: 2 out of 3
