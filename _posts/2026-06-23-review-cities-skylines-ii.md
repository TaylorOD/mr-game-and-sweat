I’d play Cities: Skylines II on a slow walk or an easy bike ride, because you can pause the simulation while you lay out a road and nobody’s asking you to react in half a second. I wouldn’t save it for a hard run where your attention is already getting negotiated by your lungs.

## Gameplay

I think the appeal is that a road you put down to solve one little traffic problem can quietly create three other problems, and that gives you something to pay attention to without making every moment urgent. You’re zoning land, building services, and trying to keep the city working as it grows, so it’s more like tending a garden where the plants can complain about the bus routes. That’s a pretty good fit for exercise when you want your brain occupied but don’t want a game shouting at you.

The planning is also where I’d expect the fun to turn into a lot of stopping and staring. A city can look like it needs a bigger road, but the traffic may be piling up because the places people need to reach are laid out badly, and I like that the game asks you to think about the whole shape of the city instead of just placing another strip of asphalt. I don’t know how often I’d correctly identify the cause on the first try. Probably not often.

Building up from an empty map is appealing because your choices have room to matter, and you get to see your little layout become a proper city. But this isn’t the sort of simulation where you can make a few choices and then coast while the game handles the interesting part. The economy and the city’s needs keep giving you reasons to stop and reconsider what you’ve built. I’d enjoy that when I’m in the mood to tinker, and find it a bit much when I’m trying to keep a steady pace.

Traffic is the mechanic I’d be most interested in, and also the one I’d be most likely to take personally. You can spend time making a road network that seems perfectly reasonable, then watch congestion tell you that the city has different opinions. That’s a useful kind of problem to work on, though I can see myself solving it by adding another road and acting surprised when that wasn’t the whole answer.

There’s enough here to keep you busy, but I wouldn’t call it an effortless distraction. You have to read what’s happening and decide what to do about it, which is more focus than a simple action game asks for. I’d put it at two out of three for gameplay. It’s interesting, but sometimes the city meeting could have been an email.

### Category Score: 2 out of 3

## Treadmill and Bike Compatibility

The pause button is doing a lot of work here, in a good way. If I’m setting down roads or trying to understand why a part of town is struggling, I can stop the simulation and take a moment instead of steering a neighborhood with one thumb while my whole body moves. Then I can let the city run while I’m exercising and watch what changes. That back and forth sounds much more manageable than trying to build continuously at full speed.

Cities: Skylines II has full controller support, which is a real advantage for this setup, but I’d still expect the menus and placement tools to take more attention than the store description makes you imagine. Controller support gets you to the controls. It doesn’t make choosing the exact shape of a road while you’re bouncing along feel automatic. I’d play with the simulation paused for the fussy building and use the treadmill or bike for watching the city run.

A bike sounds easier to me if I’m planning a lot, since I can stay seated while I work through a few decisions, but a treadmill can work too if I keep the pace gentle. I wouldn’t try to make this the game for intervals. I can picture getting halfway through a road layout, speeding up the belt, and suddenly being much more concerned with where my feet are than where the next intersection goes.

I’d also be happy to stop and think without feeling like I’m losing a match or missing a timed event. That makes the game flexible, but it does mean you can spend a lot of your workout paused and looking at the city. If you want constant movement on screen, I don’t know whether this will hold your attention. If you like solving a slow problem between stretches of walking, it could work nicely.

### Category Score: 2 out of 3

## Computer Requirements

This needs a Windows PC with a dedicated graphics card around the level of a GTX 970 or Radeon RX 480 at minimum, so I wouldn’t expect integrated graphics to be enough. I’d check the graphics card first, because a growing city is a lot to ask of a computer even when the roads are behaving.

### Category Score: 2 out of 3

## Conclusion and Score

Save Cities: Skylines II for an easy workout where you’re happy to pause, plan, and watch your choices play out. I’d suggest it to someone who likes city building and doesn’t mind doing a little thinking while moving.

### Overall Score: 2 out of 3
